# Soccernomics (2022 World Cup Edition) — Simon Kuper & Stefan Szymanski
**Format**: pdf | **Pages**: ~450 | **Depth**: study | **Use for**: transfer decisions, manager decisions, myth-busting, and "is this club actually badly run?"

## Mental Model (read first)
Football's received wisdom is a set of testable claims, and most of them are false. The book's method is: take a belief everyone holds ("the manager is the difference," "we need a marquee striker," "this club is bankrupt"), find the data, report what it says even when it is unflattering or boring. For an analyst, its highest-value contribution is a **checklist of specific, named market inefficiencies** you can run against any transfer or hiring decision.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. The thirteen secrets of the transfer market (the book's most operational asset)
Stated as the authors state them — use these as a diagnostic checklist on any signing:
1. **A new manager wastes money on transfers; don't let him.** New managers buy to signal a break with the past and to install "their" players; the outgoing manager's signings are then sold at a discount.
2. **Use the wisdom of crowds.** Decide by committee of scouts/analysts, not by one man's eye. Lyon sidelined the head coach in recruitment; the A's sidelined the manager.
3. **Stars of recent World Cups or European Championships are overvalued.** Everyone watched the same tournament; you are bidding against a fully-informed market on a small sample.
4. **Both superstars and weakest links are overvalued; your top three players matter most.** Squad-strength effort is better spent on the third-to-fifth best player than on the eleventh or on one galáctico.
5. **Certain nationalities are overvalued** (fashion cycles — Brazilian, then others). Buy from unfashionable markets.
6. **Older players are overvalued.** Clubs pay for past performance. Average striker output peaks around 25 by goals scored (Drut); the sabermetric equivalent (Bill James) put the baseball peak at 27, not the early thirties. ~40% of PL signings 2010–16 were made after peak age.
7. **Centre forwards are overvalued; goalkeepers are undervalued.** Forwards are the most expensive position in fees and wages (in Serie A 2009–14 forwards earned ~57% more than defenders, ~34% more than midfielders); keepers contribute heavily, cost less, and last longer.
8. **"Gentlemen prefer blonds": identify and abandon sight-based prejudices.** Scouts select for the look of an athlete; the bias is systematic and therefore exploitable.
9. **The best time to buy is a player's early twenties** (Lyon: the best 20–22-year-olds in their own country). Seventeen-year-olds carry too little information to judge.
10. **Sell any player when another club offers more than he is worth.** Player trading is a trading activity that produces margin, not a way to improve the team.
11. **Replace your best players before you sell them.** Early replacement avoids the transition and the panic buy.
12. **Buy players with personal problems at a discount — then actually help them.** (Clough & Taylor's method; Wenger's with Adams and Merson.)
13. **Help your players relocate.** Adaptation is a purchasable, cheap edge; Lyon employed a full-time fixer for its Brazilians.

**Term — sell-high discipline** (Taylor's rule): watch a winning side for the first signs of decline and sell the responsible players *before* buyers notice. Beane's version: always be upgrading. Kuper & Szymanski's image: players are melting assets; the club's job is to gauge the rate and sell before the value has gone.

**Anti-pattern — sentimentality as strategy.** Loyalty to an ageing favourite is a real cost, paid in wages and in an immovable contract.
**Anti-pattern — the untransferable player.** Aulas declared every international untransferable precisely to raise the price. Public loyalty statements are negotiating positions; don't read them as information.

### 2. Do managers matter? (handle with precision — this is the book's most misquoted claim)
- **The claim**: at club level, in a mature league, most managers make little difference. Wages explain almost everything at most clubs, leaving room for only a few to matter.
- **The evidence offered**: manager performance varies far more over time than player performance does (Mbappé is never "in need of sacking"); tenure is now too short for a durable effect (PL average tenure 3.5 years in 1992; the record 58 managers sacked in 2015–16 averaged 1.29 years; median top-two-tier tenure in Germany/France/Spain/Italy 2001–15 was ~350 days); and decisions are now made by staffs, not individuals — Wenger himself described the modern manager as a selector of what matters from a flood of information rather than a lone operator.
- **The caveat that makes it usable**: a few managers *do* consistently beat their wage bill. Szymanski and Peeters ranked 251 English managers with 5+ full seasons, adjusting for wage spending: Paisley top, then Ferguson, Bobby Robson, Wenger. Ferguson added value even against United's own enormous wage bill.
- **Where managers matter more**: **national teams**, where a coach can import knowledge a country lacks (Capello, Hiddink). In a league of near-perfect information like the Premier League, that gap barely exists.
- **The Wenger arc as the model of edge decay**: his early Arsenal advantage was informational (diet, sports science, knowing French-market players nobody else had scouted). Edges get copied; his later years show what happens when a manager becomes more like himself rather than adapting.
- **Anti-pattern — the great man theory.** Historians abandoned it; football journalism did not. Prefer "management team" to "manager" wherever the evidence allows.

### 3. Club finance: amortization and why "losses" mislead
- **Amortization** (post-1990s accounting): a transfer fee is written off over the length of the contract, not in one hit. A $30m fee on a three-year deal is a $10m *cost* in each of three years — **not money leaving the building** in those years.
- Scale: PL clubs in 2018–19 reported ~£5.1bn revenue and ~£424m operating losses *alongside* ~£1.3bn of amortization charges. Add amortization back and the loss becomes a cash surplus. Big clubs are **cash machines that are not profit makers**.
- **Diagnostic use**: when someone says a club "lost £X million," ask whether that is cash or accounting. Then ask what the club could *borrow* against its cash generation — that is what determines what it can actually do. Also: player registrations are near-liquid assets, which is why a paper loss rarely means distress at a big club.
- **Related**: clubs are a poor business but an extremely safe one — they almost never disappear (see also Money and Soccer on insolvency).

### 4. Other findings worth having on hand
- **City size and prizes**: big-city clubs need stars and spectacle; provincial clubs can afford patience — which is *why* Lyon's model worked there and would not have worked in Marseille. Market size shapes the strategy that is available to you, not just the money.
- **Analytics**: has genuinely moved from novelty to core function at leading clubs (Liverpool's research department under Ian Graham as the reference case). English clubs systematically overvalue Premier League experience.
- **Penalties**: a game-theory problem (mixed strategies, kicker/keeper tendencies), not a test of character.
- **Country-level performance** is largely explained by population, wealth, and experience of international football — the "core beats periphery" pattern.

## Worked Example
**Lyon, 2002–08, seven consecutive French titles.** No wage-bill dominance over Europe, no stars. The mechanism was rules 2, 9, 7, 10, 11, and 13 executed together: committee scouting under Lacombe rather than coach-led recruitment; buying the best 20–22-year-olds in their own countries; refusing to pay the centre-forward premium; selling whenever an offer exceeded valuation (Essien to Chelsea); signing the replacement six months to a year *before* the sale, so Tiago arrived at a quarter of the Essien fee; and a full-time relocation fixer so foreign signings adapted. Coaches changed and nothing changed, because stability sat with the sporting director, not the dugout. **Transferable lesson**: a club that consistently beats its wage bill is running a *process*, and the process is almost always recruitment and replacement — not motivation.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- Run any signing against the thirteen. If it violates three or more (post-tournament, post-peak, centre-forward, bought by a brand-new manager), predict it fails, and say so plainly.
- Ask **who decided** the signing before asking whether it was good. Manager-led recruitment at a big club is itself a red flag.
- Before crediting a manager, subtract the wage bill. Before sacking one, ask what the wage bill predicted — and note the club will pay again for the successor's rebuild (rule 1).
- Treat "the club is losing money" as an unfinished sentence until cash vs. accounting is settled.
- Prefer selling a year early to a year late. The cost of a year early is visible and small; a year late is invisible and total.
- When the honest answer is "the data doesn't support the interesting story," give the boring one.

## Key Takeaways
1. Thirteen named, checkable transfer inefficiencies — the fastest tool in the library for judging a signing.
2. Most club managers matter far less than assumed; a handful measurably beat their wage bill, and those are identifiable.
3. Amortization means reported losses at big clubs frequently describe accounting, not cash.
4. Sell early, replace before you sell, and never pay for a name or for past performance.
5. Market size and information availability determine which strategies are even open to a club.
